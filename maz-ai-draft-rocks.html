Overview

The hardest part of quarterly planning is starting

Ninety is built around the Entrepreneurial Operating System (EOS®) — a framework where leadership teams set quarterly priorities called “Rocks.” Rocks are supposed to be SMART: specific, measurable, actionable goals with clear milestones. In practice, they rarely started that way.

Users wrote vague Rock titles, forgot due dates, skipped milestones, and then leaned on their EOS implementers or coaches to fix them in the quarterly meeting. The feedback from coaches was consistent:

“It took a year before people consistently made SMART Rocks. Everyone pushed it off until the last minute.” — Client, confidential

The opportunity was to bring the coaching conversation into the product itself — letting Maz, Ninety’s AI agent, ask the same reflective questions a good EOS coach would ask, right at the moment of creation.

The Problem

A form captured the goal without helping define it.

The existing form asked for a title, due date, and milestones. It gave people a place to enter a goal, but little help deciding whether that goal was specific enough to work toward.

Research with users and EOS coaches exposed recurring gaps:

1
Vague, inconsistent titles — Rocks like “Improve sales” that couldn’t be measured or tracked against.
2
No SMART scaffolding — users knew Rocks should be specific and measurable but didn’t know how to get there without a prompt.
3
Clunky milestone management — the milestone creation UX added friction, so milestones were often skipped entirely.
4
Downstream coach dependency — implementers were cleaning up vague Rocks in meetings, which wasted session time and signaled a product gap.

My Role

Lead Product Designer, discovery through ship

I led design for Draft Rocks with Maz end to end, partnering with the PM and engineering team throughout:

Discovery & research — Conducted interviews with Ninety users and EOS coaches to understand the Rock creation failure modes and what good coaching looked like in practice.
UX approach & AI strategy — Defined the conversational coaching model and how it should integrate with existing Rock creation entry points without disrupting the flow for users who didn’t need it.
Chat UX & modal behavior — Designed the full conversational interface, streaming patterns, and the auto-fill behavior that moves the draft from conversation into structured form fields.
Prompt validation — Partnered with PM and engineers to test prompts, validate flows, and tune the coaching conversation before ship.

The Solution

Use the conversation to develop the draft.

I designed Maz to ask focused questions about success, measurement, and milestones, then use the answers to fill the Rock’s structured fields. People could develop the goal in conversation and review it before saving.

The interaction had to fit existing creation paths and remain optional:

Coach-like questions, not AI jargon — Maz asks the questions an EOS implementer would ask: focused, specific, leading toward measurability. The AI is a coach, not a chatbot.
SMART scaffolding through conversation — Rather than asking users to know what “SMART” means and apply it, Maz draws specificity out through the conversation and auto-populates the structured fields at the end.
Seamless entry points — The experience plugs into all three Rock creation paths: inline add, Global Create, and the linked Rock view, so the coaching is available wherever users start.
Progress preservation — Paused or abandoned drafts are preserved, so a half-finished coaching session doesn’t mean starting over.
Familiar chat patterns — Streaming text, a stop button, markdown formatting, and chat history — the same patterns users expect from modern AI tools, applied to goal creation.

Design Details

From blank form to guided conversation

The existing Rock creation entry point — an inline text input — needed to surface the option to use Maz without forcing it. I designed the affordance as a light touch: an AI assist option that appears alongside the existing inline add, so users who want to go straight to the form can, and users who want coaching can opt in.

Once in the coaching conversation, Maz works through the Rock structure progressively: first grounding the user in the goal, then specificity, then how to measure it, then breaking it into milestones. As responses come in, the structured fields auto-populate in the background — so when the conversation ends, the Rock is ready to save.

Outcomes

58% of AI-guided drafts reached a saved Rock.

58%
Completion rate for AI-guided Rock drafts — users who start the conversation finish with a saved Rock

The feature shipped as part of Maz’s broader launch. The reported completion rate was 58% for AI-guided drafts. That measures whether people finished the creation flow; it does not, on its own, establish the quality of their quarterly goals.

The reusable contribution was the interaction between conversation and structured input: preserve the draft, let people stop or resume, and keep the final save under their control. Those patterns could carry into other Maz creation experiences.

Reflection

Help people develop the goal they will own.

The important choice was the role Maz played. Asking someone to review a generated goal is different from helping them articulate their own. I chose the latter because the person setting the Rock still has to understand and carry out the commitment.

The opt-in entry point, preserved draft, stop control, and final review all supported that choice. They gave the user a way to ask for help without giving up the creation process.
