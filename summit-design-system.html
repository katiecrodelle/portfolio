Overview

Consolidation needed decisions teams could apply.

Summit brought five design systems toward a shared Figma library, Storybook, and token foundation. A component could not become a standard through visual alignment alone; teams also needed to agree on its responsibilities and behavior.

I owned component specification, documentation, and cross-functional alignment for Sheet and List Control Building Blocks. Both exposed a similar problem: teams were using the same names for different structures and interactions.

The Challenge

The disagreement was inside the component.

The audit surfaced differences in anatomy, naming, and interaction rules across product areas. A sheet could mean a brief task in one place and a multi-step workflow in another. A list row could combine layout and selection behavior without making either explicit.

I used those inconsistencies to identify the decisions the system needed to settle before teams could adopt a shared component.

My scope

Two component problems with product-wide consequences.

I focused on the rules that product teams would otherwise have to resolve repeatedly: how deep a sheet workflow can go, what happens when a keyboard opens, and whether a row navigates, selects, or simply displays information.

Interactive prototypes let engineering inspect those rules in context and challenge the parts that were ambiguous.

Workstream 01 · Sheet & Bottom Sheet

Define when a sheet should stop being a sheet.

Sheet and Bottom Sheet had separate component sets and conflicting guidance. I brought them into one model with a shared anatomy and explicit rules for mobile and desktop behavior.

The key boundary was task length. A brief contextual task could stay in a sheet; a sustained workflow needed a page. That boundary shaped drill-down, dismissal, keyboard handling, and the point where a flow should move out of the overlay.

The one rule

Brief task → sheet. Sustained workflow → full page. If the interaction requires sustained attention, complex navigation, or more than three steps of drill-down, it belongs on its own page — not in a sheet.

I documented the remaining behavior around that boundary:

Key decisions documented

1
Required ✕ Close on every sheet, every step — Not replaced by a footer Cancel button. Persists through drill-down so users always have a clear exit, regardless of where they are in the flow.
2
No stacked sheets — Drill-down replaces the body content in the same sheet rather than opening a second one. Recommended ceiling of three steps; beyond that, the flow takes over the full page.
3
Scrim is non-dismissible by default — Tapping the scrim does not close the sheet. The ✕ Close is the only guaranteed dismissal path, which prevents accidental data loss in form-heavy sheets.
4
Adaptive layout at 640px — The same component transitions between bottom sheet (mobile) and fixed 400px right panel (desktop) at the 640px breakpoint — in place, while open, without remounting.
5
Keyboard behavior specified — On mobile, the sheet resizes when the software keyboard opens. This is documented with a prototype toggle so engineering has a testable reference, not just a written spec.
6
Unified Figma organism — Deprecated the previously separate Bottom Sheet Figma set. One component set with 25 mobile variants and 1 desktop variant, all breakpoint-driven.

Prototypes as living specs

I built interactive references for mobile, desktop, and the responsive transition. Engineering could inspect keyboard resizing, drill-down, and the switch from bottom sheet to right panel rather than infer them from static frames.

Mobile sheet prototype — 9 scenarios covering short content, 90% max height, full-height expansion, persistent bar, truncated header, and drill-down through three steps with full-page handoff at step four. Includes a keyboard on/off toggle to demonstrate resize behavior.
Desktop panel prototype — Fixed 400px right panel with hover tooltip for truncated headers. No resize affordance on desktop.
Responsive transition prototype — Resize the viewport across the 640px breakpoint while the sheet is open; the component transitions between bottom sheet and right panel in place without closing.

The guidelines and review materials recorded the decision and its exceptions, giving teams a reference to use when a new product flow tested the component’s limits.

Workstream 02 · List Control Building Blocks

Separate the row’s layout from its behavior.

Campaigns, contacts, settings, and navigation used rows differently. The audit showed that treating them as one generic list component hid the distinction between arranging content and responding to input.

I separated Tile, which defines the leading, content, and trailing layout, from List Item, which defines row-level interaction states. They can be composed when needed, without making a static row behave like a selectable item.

Audit findings

1
The layout rule is right; the application isn’t — The target anatomy (leading and trailing hug content; center column flexes and truncates; 16px gap) was the right call. But the campaign list violated it by putting an overflow menu in the center column, the highest-priority anti-pattern identified in the audit.
2
Three distinct interaction models — Navigate (tap → detail), Select (tap → toggle), and Checkbox (leading checkbox owns selection). These are not stylistic variants; they define fundamentally different behaviors and should not be mixed in the same list.
3
Not every row needs List Item — Three cases: row-level interactive (Tile + List Item), control-level interactive (Tile + child control), and static/display (Tile alone). Applying List Item to non-interactive rows was causing unnecessary complexity in engineering.
4
Contacts is the gold standard for responsive — Desktop DataTable → narrow card list at breakpoint, with truncation before layout swap. This pattern became the reference implementation for how list components should handle responsive behavior across mweb and mapp.

That decision made three interaction models explicit: navigate to detail, select the row, or use a checkbox within it. The model determines where interaction belongs and which states the component must support.

I used visual comparisons and an interactive anatomy prototype to review the proposal with the team. The work turned a general request to standardize lists into specific rules for placement, truncation, and interaction.

Token Architecture

Name the role of a value.

Semantic tokens separate a component’s role from its current color or spacing value. In the component work, that meant documenting the token’s purpose rather than baking an isolated value into each variant.

The intended path was traceable from Figma variables to tokens and implemented components. Shared naming reduced ambiguity about which value a designer or engineer should use.

This is a foundation for consistent theming; it still requires implementation and validation across the components that consume it.

Accessibility

Put accessibility decisions in the shared rules.

I treated contrast, focus, keyboard handling, and dismissal as component requirements. Moving those decisions into the shared guidance gives teams a consistent starting point instead of repeating the same review on each page.

Tokens and component defaults can support accessible combinations, but they cannot guarantee accessibility on their own. The assembled interaction still needs to be tested with its content, states, and input methods.

Data Visualization

Make a chart readable beyond color.

Reporting surfaces needed ways to distinguish series without relying on hue alone. The framework paired color with marker shapes and line styles so that the encoding remained understandable when colors were difficult to distinguish.

I treated the palette and those secondary encodings as parts of the same pattern. The chart still needed to be evaluated in its final context.

Naming & documentation

Make component choices easier to interpret.

Clear component names and documented constraints help both people and AI-assisted development tools choose the intended building block. A component’s structure needs to communicate its purpose and limits.

For this work, the practical test was whether someone could distinguish Tile from List Item, choose a sheet appropriately, and find the relevant behavior without relying on a conversation with the original designer.

1
Naming as machine-readable intent — Every token, component, and pattern was named for its role and context, not its appearance. color.feedback.warning instead of color.yellow. Appearance changes. Intent doesn’t.
2
Structured documentation — Usage rationale, do/don’t examples, and decision records were written to be scannable by both humans and machine parsing. The goal was documentation that people — and agents — actually use, not documentation that exists to prove the system was documented.
3
Unambiguous component APIs — Components with well-defined props, explicit states, and documented variants reduce ambiguity for engineers and AI-assisted tooling alike. A component that requires interpretive judgment to use correctly is a component that will be used incorrectly at scale.
4
Human review gates, explicitly defined — AI can audit, tag, and surface inconsistencies in the system. What it shouldn’t do autonomously: make naming decisions, introduce new patterns, deprecate existing ones, or sign off on accessibility. Those gates exist for a reason, and Summit’s governance model defines them explicitly rather than assuming someone will catch it.

Governance

Keep the reasoning available after the review.

A component decision has to remain useful when a new team encounters a slightly different case. I paired guidelines with interactive examples and decision records so people could see the boundary and the reason behind it.

Contribution and release guidance also needed to make change predictable: record the problem, the alternatives, the intended users, and the migration path when an existing pattern changes.

Impact

A clearer boundary between reusable parts.

The sheet work established where contextual interaction belongs and where a full page is more appropriate. The list work separated layout from behavior. These decisions gave teams a more specific basis for component selection than visual similarity.

My contribution was to make those boundaries reviewable through specifications, examples, and working prototypes. Product and engineering could examine the same interaction and identify what still needed to be resolved.

Reflection

The abstraction had to survive a real product case.

A shared component becomes useful when its rules explain both a common case and an awkward one. Sheets with long drill-down flows and rows with independent child controls were the cases that clarified the model.

The audit and prototypes mattered because they exposed where a seemingly simple abstraction stopped working. Resolving that with engineering was the design work; the final component and documentation carried the decision forward.
