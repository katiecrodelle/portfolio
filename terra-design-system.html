Overview

One platform, many products, no shared language

Ninety is a business operating system — goals, meetings, scorecards, org charts, and more, each effectively its own tool. As part of a small but cross-functional design team, I co-led the creation of Terra, Ninety’s first unified design system, from the ground up.

Before Terra, each tool on the platform had its own look and feel, its own component conventions, and its own interpretation of the brand. The user experience was inconsistent. Design-to-development handoff was inefficient. And every new feature required re-litigating visual decisions that should have been settled at the system level. Terra was the answer — but building it meant more than drawing components. It meant doing the organizational work to get a system adopted and kept.

The Problem

Visual debt was slowing everyone down

The lack of a shared system showed up as three compounding problems:

1
Inconsistent visual language — the same concept (a button, a table, a modal) looked and behaved differently from tool to tool, eroding user trust and making the platform feel stitched together rather than designed.
2
Duplicated work and slow handoff — designers re-drew the same components, engineers re-built them, and every divergence created new maintenance burden on both sides. There was no single answer to “what does this component look like in its error state?”
3
No accessibility or responsive standards — without a system, accessibility was re-litigated (or skipped) per feature. Responsive behavior was inconsistent across web and mobile. Both problems compounded every sprint.

“The goal wasn’t a Figma library. It was a shared language that designers, engineers, and the product could all trust — and that would keep working as the org scaled.”

My Role

Co-leading the system: IA, tokens, lifecycle, adoption

Terra was a team effort — 6 product designers, 1 product manager, and 2 engineering leads. I co-led the initiative end-to-end, with my contributions spanning four areas that I view as inseparable in a successful 0→1 system:

Information architecture and naming — Defining the taxonomy, component hierarchy, and naming conventions that made Terra navigable and consistent. The IA work determined whether the system could be used correctly without someone explaining it, and whether new contributors could find the right primitive rather than creating a new one.
Token architecture — Establishing a semantic token system for color, spacing, typography, and interactive states — the structural layer that made theming, responsive behavior, and cross-platform consistency manageable rather than ad hoc.
Component design, documentation, and lifecycle — Designing and documenting reusable components with accessibility built in from the start, and owning the versioning, changelog, and deprecation process that kept the system alive rather than frozen.
Cross-functional alignment and adoption — Working across design, product, and engineering to align on patterns, resolve inconsistencies, and drive adoption without formal authority. The technical quality of the system mattered less than whether people actually used it.

Approach & Prioritization

Audit first. Prioritize by leverage. Ship iteratively.

A common 0→1 failure mode is starting with the components that are most fun to build rather than the ones that will have the most impact. Before we wrote a single token, I ran a component audit across Ninety’s web and mobile surfaces to identify where inconsistency was highest, where duplication was most painful, and where a shared component would immediately unblock the most teams.

That audit shaped a concrete sequenced plan — agreed on with design leadership, the PM, and engineering leads — that balanced long-term architectural correctness with near-term product needs. We didn’t build everything at once. We built the highest-leverage primitives first, shipped them, let teams adopt them, and iterated based on what broke in practice.

We structured Terra using atomic design methodology — atoms, molecules, and organisms — so every complex pattern decomposed into independently documented, reusable parts. Tokens fed atoms, atoms composed into molecules, and the whole hierarchy stayed traceable: change a token and you could reason about everything it touched.

The sequencing decision that mattered most: we shipped button, input, and color token components in the first sprint — not because they were the flashiest, but because they were used on every single screen. Early wins on high-visibility components built credibility faster than a perfect but slow-to-ship system would have.

Token Architecture

Semantic tokens that scale across themes and platforms

The token layer was the foundation everything else depended on. I structured it semantically — tokens named for their role (surface, emphasis, danger) rather than their value — which meant theming, responsive adjustments, and cross-platform consistency became configuration problems instead of redesign problems.

The token pipeline ran from Figma variables through to code, with a defined sync process to keep both sides current. The discipline paid off most visibly in component states: every interactive component — buttons, toggles, inputs, chips — drew its hover, focus, disabled, and error treatments from the same semantic ramps, so state behavior felt identical across the whole platform.

Components & Documentation

Documentation that answers the next question

A component without documentation is just a picture. For each Terra component, we wrote documentation structured around how people actually use a system: anatomy (what the parts are and which are required), behavior (hover, click, and interaction rules), writer’s tips (how to label it in plain language), variations (when to reach for primary vs. secondary vs. destructive), and update history so consumers could trust the component was maintained.

Each documentation page named a point-of-contact owner and linked directly to the Figma source — small governance details that kept documentation alive instead of decorative. The update history was non-negotiable: a system without a visible changelog is a system that engineers don’t trust to update their implementations against.

Contribution Process

Low governance tax. High trust.

One of the hardest design problems in a 0→1 system is the contribution model. Too restrictive and the system becomes a bottleneck. Too open and it fragments. We needed a process that any designer, engineer, or PM could participate in without it feeling like a committee review.

We landed on a lightweight intake process: a brief that captures the problem the component solves, what alternatives were considered, and the intended adoption scope. Any contributor could open one. The system team reviewed quickly — usually within a sprint — and responded with one of three outcomes: merge as-is, merge with modifications, or document why an existing component solved the problem instead.

01
Propose with a brief
Problem, alternatives, adoption scope

Any designer, engineer, or PM can open a contribution proposal. The bar is low: articulate the problem and what you tried before reaching for a new component. No committee. No lengthy pre-approval.

02
Fast review cycle
A decision within one sprint

The system team committed to fast turnaround. Slow review cycles are a primary driver of people skipping the process entirely and forking. Speed signals that the system is maintained.

03
Versioned releases with changelogs
Trust through transparency

Every change shipped with release notes and a deprecation window. Nothing removed without a migration path. Engineers could update their implementations against a known changelog rather than discovering breaking changes in code review.

04
Easier than the workaround
Adoption is a design problem

If using Terra costs more than copying a component from another screen, people copy. Every friction point in the process was a fork risk. We tracked where teams went off-system and treated each divergence as a signal that something needed to change.

Stakeholder Alignment

Getting 8 people to agree — and keep agreeing

The hardest part of Terra wasn’t the token architecture or the component documentation. It was getting six designers, two engineering leads, and product leadership to commit to a shared system they hadn’t asked for — and to keep using it under deadline pressure when copying a component from another screen was faster.

Each stakeholder group had a different concern, and each required a different kind of persuasion:

1
Designers: “This will slow us down.” — Six designers, six existing mental models, six ways of doing the same thing. The concern was real: switching to a shared system mid-sprint adds friction. We addressed it by shipping the highest-frequency components first — button, input, color tokens — so the payoff was visible immediately. Designers who adopted early moved faster, not slower. That visibility did the persuasion we couldn’t.
2
Engineering leads: “We need it to be stable.” — Engineering’s ask was reasonable: don’t change components without notice, don’t break implementations that are already shipped. The versioned changelog and deprecation window were designed specifically for this concern. Engineers could update against a known changelog rather than discovering breaking changes in code review. Once they trusted the release process, adoption accelerated.
3
Product leadership: “Don’t let this become a bottleneck.” — The fear was that a gated system would slow feature delivery. We countered with the audit data: re-building and re-specifying the same components was already slowing delivery — it just wasn’t visible as a system cost. The lightweight contribution process (a decision within one sprint, no committee) was our answer to the bottleneck concern in practice, not just in principle.

The insight that reframed the alignment work: influencing without authority requires making the right choice the easy choice. Every time a designer chose Terra, it had to feel faster than the alternative. The moment it felt like extra work, the system lost. That meant treating every fork — every time a team went off-system — as a product signal rather than a compliance failure.

We ran a weekly alignment meeting with design and engineering leads for the first three months — not to report status, but to surface friction. What was hard to find? What was missing? What did someone fork last sprint and why? Those answers shaped the backlog more than any roadmap we wrote upfront.

Reflection

What building Terra taught me

The organizational work is the real work

The hardest part of Terra wasn’t drawing components — it was cross-functional alignment. Six designers with six existing mental models, plus engineering leads with legitimate constraints, all had to agree on shared patterns and keep honoring them under deadline pressure. Influencing without formal authority over six designers and two engineering leads required making the system easier to use than the alternative — and making the people who contributed to it feel ownership over it.

Adoption is a design problem, not a communication problem

When teams weren’t using Terra, the instinct was to send more announcements. The real fix was always to look at what made Terra harder to use than the workaround — unclear documentation, slow contribution review, missing states — and fix that. A system that requires active promotion has a product problem, not a marketing problem.

Naming is worth the argument

We spent time debating token and component names that felt trivial in the moment. Those debates were worth it. A token named for its role rather than its appearance is documentation. It tells the next engineer, the next designer, and the next contributor what the token is for — which is more useful than knowing what hex value it resolves to.

The lifecycle matters as much as the launch

A design system without a versioning and deprecation process has an expiration date. Teams stop trusting it the first time a component changes without notice. The changelog and deprecation window discipline we built into Terra was what made teams feel safe updating their implementations — and what kept the system alive rather than frozen at v1.

Terra is also why I treat design systems leadership as foundational to product work at any scale. Consistent, well-documented components are what let teams move fast without diverging. The systems thinking I built here carries directly into how I approach design systems at Constant Contact today — including how Summit is built to serve AI-assisted workflows, not just human ones.
