Overview

The existing flow obscured an important distinction.

EOS teams evaluate both alignment with company values and fit for a specific role. Ninety’s existing Fit Check flow did not clearly support that distinction or the different responsibilities of admins, managers, and employees.

I owned the design from research through delivery, working with product and EOS practitioners to translate the framework into a clear evaluation experience.

The Problem

Values and role fit require different conversations.

Combining the two into a general performance judgment would hide the reason for a result. The design needed to preserve the framework’s distinctions while giving each role an appropriate view of the evaluation.

1
Subjective, inconsistent evaluations — Without a shared framework, two managers evaluating the same employee reach different conclusions using different criteria. People decisions become political rather than principled.
2
No visibility into development opportunities — Leaders couldn’t identify whether an employee was in the wrong seat, misaligned with values, or simply needed support — because there was no structured way to separate those diagnoses.
3
No organizational health baseline — Without structured data, teams lacked a consistent way to compare evaluations over time or review patterns across roles and departments.

I redesigned Fit Check around the language and distinctions EOS teams already used to discuss values and role fit.

My Approach

Discovery, role mapping, and EOS alignment

1. Discovery & Research

I partnered with the PM and EOS Implementers to gather early qualitative feedback, running user interviews with Admins and SLT members to understand how they currently evaluate employees, what “RPRS” means in their day-to-day language, and what friction they experienced with existing tools. This shaped both the structure of the evaluation framework and the terminology I used throughout the UI.

2. Mapping the experience by role

Each user role had distinct workflows and permissions. Rather than a one-size-fits-all interface, I designed tailored experiences for three distinct archetypes:

Admins — Configuration screens to define thresholds for Core Values and GWC ratings, control who can evaluate whom, and set the rating scale for their organization.
Managers — Evaluation dashboards with the ability to assign, complete, and track feedback. The dashboard surfaces RPRS status at a glance without requiring the manager to calculate it manually.
Employees — Simple views of their own feedback and alignment status, with plain-language context for each rating so the evaluation feels like a growth conversation, not a verdict.

Evaluation Framework

Keep the two ratings independent.

I kept Core Values and GWC—Get it, Want it, Capacity to do it—as separate ratings:

1
Core Values alignment — Rated on a + / +/- / - scale for each of the organization’s defined Core Values, using visual bars for clarity. The threshold for “Right Person” is configurable per organization, so the tool adapts to different EOS implementations rather than imposing a single standard.
2
GWC (Get it, Want it, Capacity to do it) — A Yes / +/- / No rating for each dimension of role fit. These are shown as distinct indicators rather than a combined score, so managers can distinguish between an employee who “gets it but doesn’t want it” and one who “wants it but doesn’t have the capacity.”

The ratings produce a Right Person, Right Seat status, using the organization’s configured values threshold and GWC inputs. Automating that calculation keeps the displayed status consistent with the ratings.

The most important design decision: keeping Core Values and GWC as separate, independent ratings rather than combining them into a composite score. An employee can be the right person (values-aligned) in the wrong seat (GWC not met) — and those two diagnoses lead to fundamentally different interventions. Combining them into a single score would have hidden that distinction.

The distinction remains visible in the interface. A person can align with the company’s values while struggling in a particular role; that calls for a different conversation from a values mismatch.

I built the experience with Terra components, keeping the evaluation structure distinct from the shared UI controls.

Reflection

The model mattered more than simplifying the score.

This project required resisting a superficially simpler interface. A single combined score would have been easier to display but less useful for the conversation the tool was meant to support.

Mapping the admin, manager, and employee experiences early helped me carry that distinction through configuration, evaluation, and feedback. That was the core design contribution: make a domain-specific framework usable without flattening its meaning.
