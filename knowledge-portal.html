Knowledge Portal: a shared place to learn.

Overview

Knowledge was shared, but hard to follow through.

Teams shared onboarding material and coaching resources across Slack, documents, and email. Managers could send a link, but could not easily tell who had found the right content or completed it.

I led the design of a shared learning experience through alpha, beta, and general release, working with a PM, two engineers, and another designer. The product needed to support both the person organizing knowledge and the person trying to learn from it.

The Problem

Critical knowledge was scattered and untrackable

The absence of a centralized tool created three compounding problems for Ninety organizations:

1
Inconsistent onboarding — Managers struggled to onboard team members consistently. Without a shared system, critical knowledge lived in individual people’s heads or scattered across docs no one could find.
2
No engagement visibility — Coaches wanted to assign learning materials to clients post-session but had no way to track whether content was viewed, completed, or acted on.
3
No reusable artifacts — Users needed to filter and organize content relevant to their role. Without structure, every org reinvented the same knowledge from scratch.

I needed to connect content organization, role-based access, and completion tracking in one experience.

Research & Discovery

Creation and learning needed different paths.

Interviews with clients, coaches, and internal teams showed three recurring needs: consistent onboarding, assignable follow-up material, and a way to find content relevant to a person’s role.

I mapped the Admin and Learner journeys separately. That gave the team a way to evaluate editing, permissions, and reading behavior without forcing both audiences into the same interface.

Mapping content assignment alongside Rocks and To-Dos also made the dependencies across Ninety visible before I finalized the release scope with product and engineering.

User testing rounds

Usability testing focused on the areas where people were least certain what to do next:

CTA clarity — Single dropdown vs. contextual actions. Users consistently missed the single dropdown; contextual actions performed significantly better.
Drag-and-drop — Reordering content within collections. Several participants expected keyboard-based reordering as a fallback; I added it to the design.
Information hierarchy — Whether users understood the Folder › Subject › Topic taxonomy. Early naming confused admin and learner modes; renaming to “Collection” resolved most of the ambiguity.

Key Design Decisions

Structure the content, then learn from use.

1
Dual modes: Admin vs. Learner — Creators needed robust editing tools; learners needed a clean, focused reading experience. Giving each role a distinct interface rather than trying to serve both in one view eliminated the most common points of confusion in early testing.
2
Progress indicators at topic and subtopic level — Visual completion cues at multiple levels of hierarchy made progress visible. Managers could see accountability at a glance; learners had a clear sense of where they were.
3
Scoped collaboration model — Only users with creator roles could edit shared Collections, preventing accidental changes. This felt restrictive in early feedback but turned out to be the right call — open editing created messy hierarchies in beta; scoped roles protected content quality at scale.

I worked with product and engineering to defer content analytics and draft modes from the first release. The immediate question was whether teams could create, assign, and complete useful material. Launching that loop gave me behavior to learn from before expanding the publishing workflow.

In the first 90 days, the portal reached 40,000 monthly page views, 40% account-level adoption, and more than 1,000 Collections. Those measures showed that teams were creating material as well as consuming it.

They also gave me a more specific next question. Account adoption alone did not explain what happened to someone who opened the editor and never created a Collection.

AI Iteration

The next bottleneck was creating the first Collection.

Post-launch data and follow-up interviews showed people stalling at the start of content creation. They understood the value of the portal, but turning their knowledge into a structured Collection was a separate task.

I designed a Maz-assisted flow that breaks that task into a sequence of questions. The conversation helps someone develop the content and structure together, rather than asking them to plan the whole Collection before they begin.

This was a more focused role for AI than adding a general assistant to the portal: help with the point where people were abandoning the workflow. The portal’s launch metrics are reported separately from this follow-up work.

Reflection

Launch changed the problem I needed to solve.

Early testing focused on finding, assigning, and completing content. Beta feedback led me to introduce more structure around editing and permissions. After launch, the first-Collection problem became visible.

That sequence mattered. Deferring a broader publishing tool gave me the evidence to propose an investment in a more specific creation experience.
